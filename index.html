Introduction

Project overview

Octopus arms bend, twist and wrap around objects without a rigid skeleton. Soft continuum robots try to reproduce this. In 2025 Zhanchi Wang, Nikolaos Freris and Xi Wei published SpiRobs: tentacles whose outline follows a logarithmic spiral, so that pulling one cable makes the whole body roll up into a spiral and wrap around objects of very different sizes.

SPIROBE is our Makerspace build of that idea. We imported the open SpiRobs tentacle geometry into SolidWorks, measured it, scaled a copy to 85 % so that it fits the 180 × 180 mm bed of a Bambu Lab A1 mini, and designed a compact base unit around it: a base plate, a cover, two motor brackets, two cable spools, a tentacle clamp and a pressure shoe. Two N20 gear motors wind the two cables, and an ESP32 sends PWM and direction signals to an L298N dual H-bridge.

"Robotic Hand" names the long-term goal: several tentacles working together as a gripper. This phase covers one functional tentacle. Every item on this site carries a status label, so a planned item is never mistaken for a tested one.

1.1

Background and context

Most industrial grippers are rigid: two or three fingers, a few joints and a fixed opening. They work well when the object is known in advance. They struggle with objects that are irregular, fragile or of very different sizes.

An octopus arm solves the same task very differently. It has no bones; muscle fibres arranged along, around and across the arm act on incompressible tissue, so the arm can shorten, bend and twist at any point (Kier and Smith, 1985). The arm wraps around an object and spreads the contact force over a long length.

Continuum and soft robots borrow this principle. Instead of a chain of rigid links, the body bends continuously. Many of them are driven by cables (tendons): motors stay in the base and pull cables that run along the body, so the moving part stays light and slender (Camarillo et al., 2008).

For a student Makerspace, the interesting question is whether such a robot can be built with a desktop 3D printer, hobby motors and a low-cost microcontroller, and documented well enough that someone else could repeat it.

1.3

Motivation and need

Adaptive grasping

A tentacle that curls around an object adapts to its size and shape without a separate grasp planner for every object.

Simple actuation

Two small DC gear motors and two cables are enough for planar curling in both directions. No pump, valves or molds are needed.

Makerspace tools

The body prints in TPU on an A1 mini, the chassis prints in PETG, and an ESP32 provides PWM control. Everything fits a student budget and lab.

1.5

Limitations of existing solutions and the gap

  • Fabrication barrier. Many soft arms rely on cast silicone, custom molds or pneumatic supplies, which are slow to iterate in a student lab.
  • Missing integration detail. Research papers describe the tentacle, but rarely the complete low-voltage base unit: motor mounting, spool fit, cable routing, enclosure, fuse and wiring.
  • Printer size. The source tentacle is about 250 mm long, longer than the 180 mm build volume of the A1 mini, so it cannot be printed flat at full scale.
  • Honest documentation. Student projects often present CAD renders or simulations as if they were tested hardware. We want a record that keeps these apart.

2.1

Proposed solution overview

The solution is a single tentacle mounted on a compact base unit. The tentacle is a TPU print of the SpiRobs logarithmic-spiral geometry, scaled to 85 %. Two cables run through channels on opposite sides of the tentacle. At the base, each cable is wound by its own N20 gear motor through a printed spool.

Winding cable A while releasing cable B curls the tentacle toward side A; the reverse curls it toward side B. An ESP32 sets the direction and speed of each motor through an L298N dual H-bridge. Power comes from an external 12 V adapter through a DC jack and a fuse; a buck converter supplies 5 V to the logic.

  • Mechanical: base plate, cover, two motor brackets, two spools, clamp bridge and pressure shoe.
  • Electrical: 12 V adapter, DC jack, fuse, switch (planned), buck converter, L298N, ESP32.
  • Control: open-loop PWM moves in this phase; limit switches are planned for Rev A.
2.2

Bio-inspiration: from octopus arm to logarithmic spiral

An octopus arm curls tightly at the tip and more gently near the base. SpiRobs reproduce this with a body whose outline follows a logarithmic (equiangular) spiral:

r = a · ebθ

Here r is the distance from the spiral centre, θ the turning angle, and a, b are shape constants. Each turn of the spiral is a scaled copy of the previous one, so a tentacle cut from it consists of segments that shrink by a constant ratio toward the tip.

When the cable on one side is pulled, the gaps on that side close one after another and the tentacle rolls up into the spiral it was cut from. Because the shape is self-similar, the same tentacle can wrap around large and small objects (Wang, Freris and Wei, 2025).

2.3

Working principle

1. Wind

Motor A turns its spool. Cable A shortens by about 62.8 mm per spool revolution on the first layer (π × Ø20 mm drum).

2. Curl

Tension in cable A closes the gaps on side A. The thin tip bends first, then the thicker segments follow.

3. Return

Motor A releases while motor B winds. The TPU's own elasticity and cable B bring the tentacle back and past centre.

2.6

Tentacle sizing and scaling for the printer

The source tentacle measures 249.68 × 50.58 × 28.82 mm along its principal axes (watertight mesh, 91.67 cm³). The A1 mini prints 180 × 180 × 180 mm, so the full tentacle does not fit flat on the bed.

A uniform scale of 85 % with a diagonal placement solves this:

  • Length: 0.85 × 249.68 mm = 212.2 mm
  • Maximum width: about 43.0 mm
  • Footprint on the bed: 168.4 × 168.4 mm, height 23.6 mm
  • Volume: 0.85³ × 91.67 cm³ = 56.30 cm³ (the exported file measures 56.30 cm³)
  • Margin left around the part with a 5 mm brim: about 0.8 mm per side
Trade-offUniform scaling also shrinks the cable channels and the thin flexible links by 15 %. Cable clearance and the minimum printable link thickness still have to be checked in the slicer and on the print.

The project brief lists the tentacle height as 249.48 mm; the SolidWorks point-to-point measurement reads 249.677 mm. The 0.2 mm difference depends on which vertices of the faceted mesh are picked.

2.9

Power supply and protection plan

  • The 12 V adapter stays outside the robot; only low-voltage DC enters the enclosure.
  • A fuse sits right after the DC jack positive terminal. Its rating is chosen after the motor stall current is known.
  • The buck converter is adjusted to 5.0 V and measured before the ESP32 or L298N logic is connected.
  • All grounds meet at one common 0 V point. Motor current does not flow through the ESP32.
  • The ESP32 is powered either from USB or from the external 5 V, never both at once.
Open question: motor voltageN20 motors are sold in 3 V, 6 V and 12 V versions with the same housing. The motor rail stays disconnected until the rating is confirmed from the label or the seller's listing.
Why the voltage drop mattersThe L298 datasheet gives a total bridge drop of 1.8 to 3.2 V at 1 A. From a 12 V rail a motor would see roughly 8.8 to 10.2 V, and the lost power turns into heat inside the cover.
2.10

Firmware and control logic plan

Starter sketch for the ESP32 (untested draft)

A minimal open-loop sketch written for this plan. It has not been compiled for or run on the prototype yet. It targets the Arduino-ESP32 core 3.x, whose LEDC functions attach PWM by pin number.

// SPIROBE starter sketch: NOT yet run on hardware.
// Arduino-ESP32 core 3.x LEDC API (ledcAttach / ledcWrite by pin).
const int ENA = 25, IN1 = 26, IN2 = 27;   // motor A
const int ENB = 33, IN3 = 32, IN4 = 14;   // motor B
const uint32_t PWM_HZ = 5000;   // starting value, tune on the bench
const uint8_t  PWM_BITS = 8;    // duty 0 to 255

void drive(int en, int a, int b, int speed) {   // speed: -255 to 255
  ledcWrite(en, 0);                         // zero duty before changing direction
  digitalWrite(a, speed > 0 ? HIGH : LOW);
  digitalWrite(b, speed < 0 ? HIGH : LOW);
  ledcWrite(en, abs(speed));
}

void setup() {
  int outputs[] = {ENA, IN1, IN2, ENB, IN3, IN4};
  for (int pin : outputs) { pinMode(pin, OUTPUT); digitalWrite(pin, LOW); }
  ledcAttach(ENA, PWM_HZ, PWM_BITS);
  ledcAttach(ENB, PWM_HZ, PWM_BITS);
  ledcWrite(ENA, 0);
  ledcWrite(ENB, 0);
  Serial.begin(115200);
}

void loop() {   // 'a' curls toward A, 'b' toward B, any other key stops
  if (!Serial.available()) return;
  char command = Serial.read();
  if (command == 'a')      { drive(ENB, IN3, IN4, 0); drive(ENA, IN1, IN2, 160); }
  else if (command == 'b') { drive(ENA, IN1, IN2, 0); drive(ENB, IN3, IN4, 160); }
  else                     { drive(ENA, IN1, IN2, 0); drive(ENB, IN3, IN4, 0); }
  delay(400);                               // short timed move (open loop)
  drive(ENA, IN1, IN2, 0);
  drive(ENB, IN3, IN4, 0);
}

2.12

Assembly and integration plan

Planned
  1. Clean every printed part and test-fit; clear or tap pilot holes instead of forcing screws.
  2. Slide each spool onto its N20 D-shaft and lock it with an M2 set screw on the flat. Check free rotation.
  3. Fix each motor to its bracket (M1.6 × 4, provisional) and each bracket to the base (M3 × 12, washer and nut under the base).
  4. Mount the L298N on its four posts (M3 × 8 with insulating washers) and place the ESP32 against its corner stops with a zip tie.
  5. Wire the circuit with the adapter unplugged. Set the buck output to 5.0 V before connecting the ESP32 and L298N logic.
  6. Route each cable from the underside of its spool drum, through its Ø2 mm guide hole, into its tentacle channel, and anchor it at the tip.
  7. Seat the tentacle root in the U-cradle, add the pressure shoe and clamp bridge, and tighten without crushing the TPU.
  8. Run each motor alone without cables at low PWM, then with cables at low pretension.
  9. Fit the cover with four M3 × 12 screws once the motion and temperature checks pass.

3.7

Wiring and power

Rev A drawn

The circuit has two supply levels. 12 V from the adapter goes through the DC jack, the fuse and the main switch to the buck converter and to the L298N motor supply. The buck converter makes 5.0 V for the ESP32 and the L298N logic. The ESP32 sends three signals to the driver for each motor: one PWM enable line for speed and two direction lines.

  • One common 0 V point for every module; motor current never returns through the ESP32.
  • The ESP32 runs from the buck converter or from USB, never both at once.
  • The L298N 5V-EN jumper is removed because its logic is fed from the buck converter.
  • The motor supply stays disconnected until the N20 voltage rating is confirmed.

4.1

Project summary

SPIROBE is a cable-driven soft tentacle based on the SpiRobs logarithmic-spiral design. Two cables run through the tentacle; two small gear motors wind them in turn, so the tentacle curls toward one side and then the other. The project covers one functional tentacle as the first unit of a future multi-tentacle robotic hand.

4.8

Solutions and lessons learned

Design
  • Measure the reference geometry before designing around it. The root width set the cradle, the clamp and the cover opening.
  • Check the printer envelope at the start. Scaling to fit changes every interface that touches the part.
  • Name features and dimensions. The rebuild test on the base plate and cover was only practical because the sketch dimensions had names.
Verification
  • A static interference check is quick and catches layout mistakes, but it is not a fit test.
  • Measure the logic rail before connecting anything, and keep the motor supply off until the motor rating is known.
  • Label every image by its source. It keeps the record honest about what was built and what was only drawn.
4.9

Limitations

  • Planar bending only. Two cables bend the tentacle in one plane; spatial bending needs three or more cables.
  • Open-loop control. No sensing of cable tension, tentacle position or grip force; moves are timed.
  • 85 % scale. Shorter reach, thinner links and smaller cable channels than the published design.
  • Bipolar driver. The L298N loses 1.8 to 3.2 V at 1 A and runs warm.
  • Unknown motor data. Voltage rating, gear ratio, torque and current are still to be confirmed.
  • CAD-only checks. Placements are fixed (no motion study), and masses are solid-material estimates.
  • No experimental data yet. No performance figure on this page comes from a test.
4.10

Future scope

Three-cable tentacle

Spatial bending using three cables at 120°, based on the hole pattern sketched in Step 01.

Multi-tentacle hand

Two or three tentacles on a common base to grasp objects together: the robotic hand the project is named for.

Sensing and feedback

Encoder versions of the N20 motors, cable tension or current sensing, and limit switches for safe winding.

Better motor driver

TB6612FNG or a MOSFET H-bridge for a lower voltage drop and less heat than the L298N.

Wireless control

A Wi-Fi web page or Bluetooth Low Energy app using the radios already on the ESP32.

Full-size tentacle

Print the full-length tentacle in sections, or on a larger printer, and compare its reach and grip with the 85 % version.
